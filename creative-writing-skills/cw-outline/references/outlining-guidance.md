# Outlining Guidance

This reference file provides guidance for creating effective story outlines at all levels — act, chapter, and scene. Use these principles when building outlines for the `/outline` skill.

---

## What Makes a Good Scene Outline

Every scene outline must contain these five elements:

### 1. Goal (Want)

What does the POV character want in this scene? This should be:
- **Specific**: Not "she wants to be happy" but "she wants Marcus to agree to help her"
- **Immediate**: Something achievable within the scene, not a life-long dream
- **Active**: Something the character is pursuing, not just experiencing

### 2. Conflict (Obstacle)

What's preventing the character from achieving their goal? The conflict should be:
- **Real**: Something that genuinely opposes, not just a minor inconvenience
- **Escalating**: In a sequence of scenes, later conflicts should feel more challenging
- **Varied**: Mix external obstacles (other characters, circumstances) with internal obstacles (fears, doubts)

### 3. Outcome

What actually happens? Three possibilities:

| Outcome | Description | Use When |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| **Success** | Character achieves their goal | Victory feels earned; advances plot significantly |
| **Failure** | Character fails completely | Defeat is devastating; sets up reversal or regrouping |
| **Partial Success** | Character achieves something, but at a cost or with complications | Most common; creates forward momentum with tension |

**Partial success is the most common and often the most interesting** — it gives you both a win and a problem.

### 4. Emotional Shift

How does the POV character's emotional state change from the start of the scene to the end?

- Every scene should have an emotional **shift**, not just emotional **content**
- The shift should be **specific and trackable**: "suspicion → trust" not just "changes"
- Emotional shifts drive pacing — alternating high-tension and low-tension scenes creates rhythm

**Example emotional arcs:**
- Relief → fear (when the safe option turns out to be dangerous)
- Confidence → doubt (when a plan starts to unravel)
- Anger → guilt (after lashing out at the wrong person)
- Hope → resignation (when accepting an unpleasant truth)

### 5. Beats (Short Action Descriptions)

Beats are the building blocks of a scene. Each beat is:

- **1 sentence** — not a paragraph, not multiple actions
- **Action-focused** — describes what happens, not internal thought
- **Prose-ready** — can be directly translated into a paragraph of prose
- **Active voice** — subject performs verb, no passive construction

**Good beat:**
> Aria enters the inn, scans the room — tense, aware she's being watched

**Bad beats (too long, too detailed, not actionable):**
> Aria enters the inn, scans the room with barely concealed anxiety, noticing that the usual travelers are absent and replaced by unfamiliar faces that seem to be watching her every move
>
> *"— too long, not a single action"*

> Marcus is silent for a long moment before speaking.
>
> *"— too vague, doesn't tell us what happens"*

## Common Chapter Structure Patterns

### Scene and Sequel

Classic pattern alternating between action and reflection:

```
Scene: Goal → Conflict → Disaster (failure or partial failure)
Sequel: Reaction → Dilemma → Decision (leads into next scene)
```

**Example:**
- **Scene**: Aria tries to convince Marcus to help her → Marcus refuses → Aria is thrown out
- **Sequel**: Aria reels from rejection → She considers giving up → She decides to find another way

### Action and Reaction

Similar but emphasizes the rhythm more:

```
Action: External events unfold (beats 1-3)
Reaction: Character processes what happened (beat 4)
Decision: Character commits to next step (beat 5)
```

### Montage

Multiple short beats compressed into one scene (useful for transitions):

```
Beat 1 → Beat 2 → Beat 3 → Beat 4
(Aria travels through the forest, encountering X, then Y, then Z)
```

Use montages for:
- Travel sequences
- Training scenes
- Gathering information
- Time jumps

### Revelation Sequence

Slow reveal of information, often used in mystery or investigation:

```
Hook → Question → Investigation → Evidence → Question (new) → [loop]
```

Each piece of evidence raises a new question, pulling the reader forward.

### Confrontation

Face-to-face conflict with escalating stakes:

```
Opening parry → First thrust → Counter → Escalation → Final clash
```

Useful for arguments, battles, negotiations, and debates.

## Mapping Structure Templates to Outlines

### Save the Cat (15 Beats)

| Beat | Act | Where It Lives |
|------|-----|----------------|
| 1. Opening Image | Act 1 | Before chapter 1 or in chapter 1 |
| 2. Theme Stated | Act 1 | Often in dialogue or a character observation |
| 3. Setup | Act 1 | Chapters 1-2 (introduce world, characters) |
| 4. Catalyst | Act 1 | End of chapter 2 or start of chapter 3 |
| 5. Debate | Act 1 | Chapter 3-4 (should the protagonist act?) |
| 6. Break into Act Two | Act 2 | Decision point; chapter 4-5 |
| 7. B Story | Act 2 | Often a subplot; runs parallel to main story |
| 8. Fun and Games | Act 2 | The promise of the premise; chapters 5-8 |
| 9. Midpoint | Act 2 | False victory or false defeat; chapter 8-9 |
| 10. Bad Guys Close In | Act 2 | External and internal threats escalate |
| 11. All Is Lost | Act 2 | Lowest point; often includes a whiffle |
| 12. Dark Night of the Soul | Act 2 | Protagonist processes the loss |
| 13. Break into Act Three | Act 3 | Decision to try again; chapter 13-14 |
| 14. Finale | Act 3 | Climax and resolution; chapters 14-15 |
| 15. Final Image | Act 3 | Image that contrasts with beat 1 |

### Three-Act Structure

```
Act 1 (25%): Setup → Inciting Incident → Break into Act 2
Act 2 (50%): Rising Action → Midpoint → Complications → Break into Act 3
Act 3 (25%): Climax → Resolution → Denouement
```

Map chapters to acts based on your target word count. For a 80,000-word novel:
- Act 1: ~20,000 words (chapters 1-4)
- Act 2: ~40,000 words (chapters 5-13)
- Act 3: ~20,000 words (chapters 14-17)

### Hero's Journey (12 Steps)

| Step | Where It Lives |
|------|----------------|
| 1. Ordinary World | Act 1, chapter 1 |
| 2. Call to Adventure | Act 1, catalyst |
| 3. Refusal of the Call | Act 1, debate |
| 4. Meeting the Mentor | Act 1 or early Act 2 |
| 5. Crossing the Threshold | Break into Act 2 |
| 6. Tests, Allies, Enemies | Act 2, first half |
| 7. Approach to Inmost Cave | Before midpoint |
| 8. Ordeal | Midpoint |
| 9. Reward | Post-midpoint |
| 10. The Road Back | Break into Act 3 |
| 11. Resurrection | Climax |
| 12. Return with Elixir | Resolution |

## Red Flags in Outlines

### Scene-Level Problems

**Scene without conflict:**
> Aria walks into town. She buys apples. She walks home.

*There's activity but no tension. A scene needs opposition.*

**Flat emotional arc:**
> Aria is angry throughout the entire scene. She gets more angry. The scene ends with her still angry.

*Emotion needs to move, not just persist.*

**Same emotional direction consecutively:**
> Scene 1 ends: fear
> Scene 2 ends: more fear
> Scene 3 ends: intense fear

*Readers need emotional variety. Alternate tension and relief.*

**Beats that aren't beats:**
> Marcus considers whether to tell her the truth. He decides to wait.

*These are thoughts, not actions. Beats should describe observable behavior.*

### Chapter-Level Problems

**Chapter that's really two chapters:**
> Chapter 3 covers Aria's morning, then the afternoon, then evening, then jumps to Marcus's story, then back to Aria — but wait, now we're in a new location entirely.

*If a chapter has multiple distinct scene groups with different focus characters, it might be multiple chapters.*

**Chapter with no clear purpose:**
> This chapter exists to transition from the inn to the forest. That's it.

*Transitions can be shorter. If a chapter's only job is to move characters from A to B, consider compressing it or combining it with adjacent chapters.*

### Act-Level Problems

**Act One doesn't set up Act Two's central conflict:**
*The inciting incident should make the protagonist's goal in Act Two inevitable or highly likely.*

**Act Two has no midpoint:**
*Without a midpoint (false victory or false defeat), Act Two sags in the middle. The midpoint re-engages the reader.*

**Act Three feels rushed:**
*If your climax feels abrupt, your Act Three might need more chapters — or Act Two might be too long.*

## Scene Sequence Design

### The Promise of the Premise

Whatever your story's central "hook" is — the mystery, the romance, the battle, the heist — the midpoint should deliver the most complete realization of that promise. By midpoint, the reader should feel "yes, this is the story I signed up for."

### Pacing Alternation

Alternate scene types for rhythm:

```
High tension → Low tension → Medium → High → Low → Climax
```

Long sequences of high-tension scenes exhaust readers. Long sequences of low-tension scenes bore them.

### Question Raising

Each scene should:
1. Raise a question (explicit or implicit)
2. Answer it (or partially answer it)
3. Raise a new question

This question-answer-new question pattern keeps readers engaged.

## Practical Beat Writing Tips

### Use Active, Specific Verbs

| Instead of... | Use... |
|--------------|--------|
| "Aria thinks about Marcus" | "Aria avoids Marcus's gaze" |
| "Marcus is angry" | "Marcus slams his palm on the table" |
| "The room goes quiet" | "Conversation dies mid-sentence" |

### Include Sensory Detail in Beats

```
Weak: She notices the symbol.
Better: She catches the symbol carved into the doorframe — a serpent eating its tail.
```

### Include Emotional Stakes in Beats

```
Weak: Marcus refuses to help.
Better: Marcus refuses — and the one person who could change his mind just walked out.
```

### Beats Shouldn't Reference Each Other

Each beat should be independently understandable. The reader experiences them sequentially, so each beat must establish its own context.

```
Weak: She shows him the carving. He recognizes it.
Better: She shows him the carving — a serpent eating its tail. Marcus goes pale.
```

## Scene Length Guidance

| Scene Type | Typical Word Count | Typical Beat Count |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Short scene | 500-1,000 words | 3-4 beats |
| Standard scene | 1,000-2,000 words | 5-7 beats |
| Complex scene | 2,000-3,000 words | 7-10 beats |
| Montage | 300-800 words | 4-8 short beats |

A chapter is typically 2-5 scenes, totaling 3,000-8,000 words.

## Revision Triggers

When reviewing an outline, watch for these as signs that revision is needed:

1. **A scene goal that's the same as the chapter goal** — the scene should have its own, smaller goal
2. **Outcome always matching goal** — success should be rarer than partial success or failure
3. **Every character wanting the same thing** — conflict needs opposition
4. **POV character never wrong** — internal conflict means the character often misunderstands
5. **All scenes at maximum intensity** — rhythm requires variation
6. **Beats that tell us what characters think** instead of what they do
