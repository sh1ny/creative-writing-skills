# Story Structure Templates Reference

This reference contains seven proven story structure templates. Use this when the user doesn't have a preferred structure, or when they want to understand their options.

---

## 1. Save the Cat (Blake Snyder)

**Origin:** Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat!®* (2005), based on screenwriting but applicable to novels.

**Description:** A 15-beat structure optimized for commercial appeal. Each beat is a specific story moment with a defined purpose. Popular for its clarity and practicality.

### The 15 Beats

| # | Beat Name | Description |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| 1 | **Opening Image** | A powerful visual that establishes the tone, mood, and scale of the story. Shows the "before" state of the protagonist's world. |
| 2 | **Theme Stated** | Someone (often a supporting character) states the story's theme, often to the protagonist who doesn't yet understand it. Seeds the thematic idea. |
| 3 | **Setup** | Introduces the protagonist, their world, and the people around them. Establishes their "flaw" or "wound" that needs healing. |
| 4 | **Catalyst** | Something happens that disrupts the status quo. A phone call, an event, a revelation — the inciting incident that starts the story proper. |
| 5 | **Debate** | The protagonist questions whether to act, what to do, or if they can handle the challenge. Internal conflict as they weigh options. |
| 6 | **Break into Two** | The protagonist makes a decision and enters the "new world" of the story. They commit to the journey, crossing the threshold. |
| 7 | **B Story** | A subplot begins, often a love story or friendship. This provides relief from the main tension and often carries the theme. |
| 8 | **Fun and Games** | The central conflict is explored. The protagonist pursues their goal, faces obstacles, and has their key experiences. This is the "promise of the premise." |
| 9 | **Midpoint** | A major event (false victory or false defeat) that raises the stakes and changes the protagonist's approach. Marks the shift from reactiveness to proactiveness. |
| 10 | **Bad Guys Close In** | External pressures mount (villain closes in) and internal pressures grow (relationships strain). Things go wrong on multiple fronts. |
| 11 | **All Is Lost** | The lowest point. A major loss or defeat, often accompanied by a "whiff of death" — a moment that symbolizes the worst-case scenario. |
| 12 | **Dark Night of the Soul** | The protagonist processes their loss. They are at their lowest emotionally. Questions about whether to continue arise. |
| 13 | **Break into Three** | A new idea, inspiration, or determination emerges. The protagonist finds the solution or resolves to try again, often from the B Story. |
| 14 | **Finale** | The climax. All threads come together. The protagonist confronts the antagonist (external) and their internal flaw (internal). They choose who they will be. |
| 15 | **Final Image** | The opposite of the Opening Image. Shows the "after" state — how the protagonist and their world have changed. |

### Key Characteristics

- **Emphasis on choice:** The protagonist's decision at "Break into Two" commits them to the story.
- **B Story carries theme:** The subplot often illustrates the theme the main plot explores.
- **Midpoint pivot:** The midpoint shifts the protagonist from reactive to proactive.
- **All Is Lost / Dark Night:** Two distinct beats — the external loss and the internal despair.
- **Finale balance:** The climax must resolve both external conflict AND internal transformation.

### When to Use

Commercial fiction, genre fiction, stories with clear protagonist arcs, stories with romantic or friendship subplots.

## 2. Story Grid (Shawn Coyne)

**Origin:** Shawn Coyne's *Story Grid* (2015), a systematic approach to story analysis and writing.

**Description:** A rigorous, analytical structure based on the "Five Commandments of Storytelling" applied at both scene and act levels. Emphasizes cause-and-effect chain of events and genre-specific conventions.

### The Five Commandments (Per Scene)

| Commandment | Description |
|-------------|-------------|
| **Inciting Incident** | The moment that disrupts equilibrium and forces the character to react. Every scene needs one. |
| **Progressive Complication** | The character attempts to handle the inciting incident but complications arise. Tension increases. |
| **Crisis** | The moment of decision — the character must choose a course of action, often between two bad options. |
| **Climax** | The execution of that choice — the moment of highest intensity in the scene where the choice plays out. |
| **Resolution** | The aftermath — how the world settles after the climax. Often establishes the new equilibrium (or lack thereof). |

### Story Grid at Act Level

Apply the Five Commandments to major story divisions:

| Story Unit | Inciting Incident | Progressive Complication | Crisis | Climax | Resolution |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------------|--------|--------|------------|
| **Overall Story** | The Big Gun (global inciting incident) | Rising action | Midpoint choice | Global climax | Denouement |
| **Act 1** | Hook | Setup complications | Break into Two | — | — |
| **Act 2A** | (from Break into Two) | Rising complications | Midpoint crisis | Midpoint climax | — |
| **Act 2B** | (from Midpoint) | Complications escalate | Crisis (All Is Lost) | — | — |
| **Act 3** | (from Crisis) | Final complications | Final choice | Final climax | Resolution |

### Key Characteristics

- **Cause-and-effect chain:** Every scene is a complete mini-story following the Five Commandments.
- **Genre-specific:** Different genres have different "value shifts" (what changes from bad to good or vice versa).
- **Layered structure:** The Five Commandments apply at scene, sequence, act, and global story levels.
- **Observer's journey vs. actor's journey:** The protagonist experiences change; the reader witnesses it.
- **Object of desire:** Every story has a central "want" that drives the protagonist.

### Genre Conventions

Story Grid identifies genre by the:
- **External genre** (the type of story: thriller, romance, horror, etc.)
- **Internal genre** (the theme/value being explored: justice, love, mortality, etc.)

Each genre has specific "obligatory scenes" and "conventions" that readers expect.

### When to Use

Analytical writers who want a systematic approach, writers who enjoy understanding genre conventions, revision and critique work.

## 3. Hero's Journey (Joseph Campbell / Christopher Vogler)

**Origin:** Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), adapted for screenwriting by Christopher Vogler in *The Writer's Journey* (1992).

**Description:** A mythic structure based on patterns found across world mythology. The protagonist leaves the ordinary world, undergoes transformation, and returns changed. Also called the "monomyth."

### The 12 Stages

| # | Stage Name | Description |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| 1 | **Ordinary World** | The hero's normal life before the story begins. Establishes who they are and what's missing. |
| 2 | **Call to Adventure** | Something disrupts the Ordinary World. The hero is presented with a challenge or quest. |
| 3 | **Refusal of the Call** | The hero hesitates or refuses, expressing fear of the unknown. Establishes stakes. |
| 4 | **Meeting the Mentor** | The hero encounters a guide who provides wisdom, training, or道具 (tools) for the journey. |
| 5 | **Crossing the Threshold** | The hero commits to the adventure and enters the Special World. No going back. |
| 6 | **Tests, Allies, Enemies** | The hero faces challenges, makes friends and foes, and learns the rules of the Special World. |
| 7 | **Approach to the Inmost Cave** | The hero prepares for the major challenge ahead. Approaches the central crisis point. |
| 8 | **The Ordeal** | The hero faces their greatest fear or most dangerous challenge. A death-and-rebirth moment. |
| 9 | **Reward (Seizing the Sword)** | The hero survives and gains something — a treasure, knowledge, or reconciliation. |
| 10 | **The Road Back** | The hero begins the return to the Ordinary World, but complications arise. |
| 11 | **Resurrection** | The final test where the hero must use everything they've learned. A climax that transforms. |
| 12 | **Return with the Elixir** | The hero returns to the Ordinary World, transformed, bringing something to benefit all. |

### Key Characteristics

- **Death and rebirth:** The Ordeal often involves a symbolic or literal near-death.
- **Transformation focus:** The hero is fundamentally changed by the journey.
- **Mentor archetype:** Wise figures help the hero prepare.
- **Shapeshifters and threshold guardians:** Antagonists and obstacles often follow mythic patterns.
- **Return essential:** The journey isn't complete until the hero returns with something learned.

### Vogler's 7 Character Archetypes

| Archetype | Role |
|-----------|------|
| **Hero** | The protagonist who undergoes transformation |
| **Mentor** | Provides wisdom, training, or magical aids |
| **Threshold Guardian** | Tests the hero's worthiness |
| **Herald** | Issues the Call to Adventure |
| **Shapeshifter** | Changes in unpredictable ways (often antagonist) |
| **Shadow** | The villain or embodiment of the hero's fears |
| **Trickster** | Provides comic relief and disruption |

### When to Use

Fantasy, adventure, mythic stories, transformation narratives, stories with clear protagonist journeys, hero's journey adaptation for any genre.

## 4. Three-Act Structure

**Origin:** Ancient dramatic theory (Aristotle's *Poetics*), formalized in modern screenwriting and fiction.

**Description:** The most classic dramatic structure. The story divides into three parts: Setup, Confrontation, and Resolution. Simple, fundamental, and widely applicable.

### The Three Acts

| Act | Description | Approximate Length |
|-----|-------------|-------------------|
| **Act I: Setup** (~25%) | Establishes characters, world, and status quo. Introduces the conflict. Ends with the inciting incident. | 1-4 chapters |
| **Act II: Confrontation** (~50%) | The protagonist pursues their goal, facing escalating obstacles. Ends with the climax. | 4-10 chapters |
| **Act III: Resolution** (~25%) | The climax and its aftermath. The conflict is resolved. | 1-4 chapters |

### Key Turning Points

| Turning Point | Location | Description |
|---------------|----------|-------------|
| **Inciting Incident** | End of Act I | The event that launches the story proper. |
| **Midpoint** | Middle of Act II | A major shift that raises stakes or changes the game. |
| **Crisis/Climax** | End of Act II / Act III | The lowest point leading to the final confrontation. |
| **Resolution** | End of Act III | Aftermath of the climax. Denouement. |

### Detailed Breakdown

**Act I: Setup (approximately 25% of story)**
- Opening scene establishes tone, setting, and character
- Introduce protagonist in their ordinary world
- Establish theme or moral question (often subtly)
- Inciting incident disrupts the status quo
- Protagonist reluctantly or eagerly accepts the challenge
- **Plot Point 1:** Crossing the threshold — protagonist commits to the story

**Act II: Confrontation (approximately 50% of story)**

*First half (rising action):*
- Protagonist actively pursues their goal
- Introduce allies and antagonists
- Obstacles and complications arise
- Protagonist experiences successes and failures
- **Midpoint:** Major revelation, reversal, or decision point

*Second half (falling action / rising complications):*
- Stakes escalate
- The situation grows more dire
- Protagonist faces setbacks
- The antagonist gains advantage
- **Plot Point 2:** Crisis — the darkest moment before the climax

**Act III: Resolution (approximately 25% of story)**
- Climax — the final confrontation
- Protagonist faces the antagonist or central conflict
- The thematic question is answered (implicitly or explicitly)
- Resolution — aftermath and new equilibrium

### When to Use

Universal — works for virtually any story. The foundation upon which other structures are built.

## 5. Four-Act Structure

**Origin:** An expansion of Three-Act Structure, common in television (especially prestige TV) and increasingly used in novels.

**Description:** Divides the story into four acts with a midpoint that fully splits Act II into two distinct halves. Creates more natural pause points and allows for more complex plotting.

### The Four Acts

| Act | Description | Approximate Length |
|-----|-------------|-------------------|
| **Act I** (~15-20%) | Setup. Ends with first major plot point. | Varies |
| **Act IIA** (~20-25%) | Rising action. Ends with midpoint. | Varies |
| **Act IIB** (~20-25%) | Rising action continues from midpoint. Ends with third act turn. | Varies |
| **Act III** (~30-35%) | Climax and resolution. | Varies |

### Key Turning Points

| Beat | Location | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Opening Image** | Scene 1 | Visual establishing the story's tone and protagonist's starting state |
| **Theme Stated** | Act I | Often subtle — a question posed or observation made |
| **Plot Point 1** | End of Act I | First major turning point; protagonist commits to journey |
| **Pinches / Midpoint** | Middle of Act IIA | External pressure increases (first "pinch") |
| **Midpoint** | End of Act IIA | Major reversal or revelation; protagonist becomes more proactive |
| **Pinches / Midpoint 2** | Middle of Act IIB | Second "pinch" — antagonist gains ground |
| **Plot Point 3** | End of Act IIB | Darkest moment or major defeat before climax |
| **Climax** | Act III | Final confrontation and resolution |
| **Final Image** | Last scene | Opposite of opening image — shows transformation |

### Why Four Acts?

| Advantage | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Better pacing** | Shorter acts create more frequent turning points, maintaining tension |
| **Midpoint emphasis** | The midpoint is a full act break, giving it more weight |
| **Television-friendly** | Natural act breaks for episode structure |
| **Subplot space** | Act breaks can mark when subplots intensify or resolve |
| **More complexity** | Allows for multiple major reversals rather than one midpoint |

### When to Use

Stories with multiple subplots, complex narratives, serial fiction, literary fiction, stories where you want more frequent turning points, or when adapting to a serialized format.

## 6. Seven-Point Story Structure (Dan Wells)

**Origin:** Dan Wells, based on ideas from Brandon Sanderson and others.

**Description:** A focused structure that identifies seven essential plot points. Emphasizes the protagonist's internal and external arcs and the necessity of failure before success.

### The Seven Points

| # | Point Name | Description |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| 1 | **Hook** | The opening. Establishes the protagonist, their flaw, and their ordinary world. Also called "Ordinary World." |
| 2 | **Plot Turn 1** | The inciting incident. The event that pulls the protagonist into the story. Called the "Call" but must be external and undeniable. |
| 3 | **Pinch Point 1** | The antagonist exerts pressure. The protagonist fails because of their flaw. Shows what the antagonist is capable of. |
| 4 | **Midpoint** | The protagonist stops reacting and starts acting. A false victory or false defeat that shifts them from passive to active. |
| 5 | **Pinch Point 2** | The antagonist exerts more pressure. The protagonist fails again, often in a worse way. Stakes are raised to near-despair. |
| 6 | **Plot Turn 2** | The "Break into Three" moment. The protagonist realizes the truth, overcomes their flaw, and commits to the final confrontation. |
| 7 | **Resolution** | The climax. The protagonist uses their new understanding to defeat the antagonist (external) and become who they need to be (internal). |

### Visual Map

```
                    PLOT TURN 2
                         │
                         ▼
    ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐
    │                                      │
    │          PINCH POINT 2               │
    │     (Antagonist pressure ↑↑)         │
    │              │                       │
    │              ▼                       │
    │         MIDPOINT                     │
    │    (Protagonist turns active)       │
    │              │                       │
    │              ▼                       │
    │         PINCH POINT 1                │
    │     (Antagonist pressure ↑)          │
    │              │                       │
    │              ▼                       │
    │         PLOT TURN 1                  │
    │    (Inciting incident)              │
    │              │                       │
    │              ▼                       │
    │            HOOK                      │
    │    (Ordinary world)                  │
    │                                      │
    └──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Antagonist's Role

| Pinch Point | Antagonist Action | Purpose |
|-------------|-------------------|---------|
| **Pinch 1** | Shows power/threat early | Establishes antagonist capability; protagonist fails due to flaw |
| **Pinch 2** | Escalates pressure late | Near-despair; protagonist fails again due to flaw |

The antagonist should be active and threatening throughout, not just at the climax.

### Key Characteristics

- **Failure is essential:** The protagonist must fail before they succeed.
- **Flaw-driven:** Each failure relates to the protagonist's internal flaw.
- **Antagonist pressure:** The antagonist must exert pressure at both pinch points.
- **Midpoint pivot:** The protagonist shifts from reactive to proactive.
- **Plot Turn 2 is about growth:** The final turn is internal transformation, not just external change.

### When to Use

Thriller, horror, action-adventure, character-driven stories with clear internal arcs, stories where the protagonist's growth is central.

## 7. Freytag's Pyramid

**Origin:** Gustav Freytag's *Die Technik des Dramas* (1863), analyzing classical drama.

**Description:** A five-part dramatic structure visualizes as a pyramid. While designed for five-act plays, the model applies to three-act stories with a rising and falling action.

### The Five Parts

| Part | Pyramid Position | Description |
|------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Exposition** | Base (left side) | Introduction of characters, setting, and situation. Establishes the status quo. |
| **Rising Action** | Left slope | A series of complications and conflicts build tension. Events unfold logically. |
| **Climax** | Peak | The turning point — the moment of highest tension. The decisive moment. |
| **Falling Action** | Right slope | Events follow logically from the climax. The conflict moves toward resolution. |
| **Dénouement (Catastrophe/Resolution)** | Base (right side) | The aftermath. The conflict is resolved and new equilibrium is established. |

### Visual Pyramid

```
        CLIMAX
          /\
         /  \
        /    \
       /      \
      /        \
     /          \
    /            \
EXP/RISING     FALLING/DENOUEMENT
```

### Detailed Breakdown

**Exposition (approximately 10-15%)**
- Introduce main characters
- Establish setting (time and place)
- Reveal relevant background
- Show normal life / status quo
- Introduce theme or conflict seed

**Rising Action (approximately 50%)**
- Complications arise
- Conflicts develop
- Tension builds
- Subplots introduced (if any)
- Stakes increase
- All leading to the climax

**Climax (approximately 10-15%)**
- The turning point
- Moment of highest tension
- Central conflict is decided
- Character faces their greatest challenge
- Irreversible change occurs

**Falling Action (approximately 15-20%)**
- Consequences of the climax unfold
- Events resolve in the new reality
- Subplots conclude
- Secondary characters' fates resolved

**Dénouement (approximately 5-10%)**
- Final resolution
- New equilibrium established
- Loose ends tied
- Message/theme clarified (implicitly)
- Story complete

### Comparison with Three-Act Structure

| Freytag | Three-Act Equivalent |
|---------|-------------------|
| Exposition | Act I (Setup) |
| Rising Action | First half of Act II |
| Climax | Second half of Act II / Beginning of Act III |
| Falling Action | Act III (Resolution) |
| Dénouement | Act III (Resolution) |

### When to Use

Classical drama, literary fiction, straightforward narratives, stories with clear dramatic arc, tragedy (where catastrophe = tragic ending), or comedy (where catastrophe = resolution of romantic/comedic entanglements).

## Choosing a Structure

| If your story... | Consider... |
|-------------------|-------------|
| Is commercial/genre fiction with clear protagonist arc | Save the Cat |
| You want analytical rigor and genre focus | Story Grid |
| Is mythic, transformation-focused | Hero's Journey |
| Is relatively straightforward | Three-Act Structure |
| Has complex subplots or serial structure | Four-Act Structure |
| Has strong antagonist presence and failure-then-success arc | Seven-Point |
| Is classical drama with clear rising/falling action | Freytag's Pyramid |

All structures share common elements: setup, inciting incident, rising complications, climax, and resolution. Choose the one that most naturally fits your story's needs.

## Generating a Skeleton Outline

When the user selects a structure, generate a skeleton outline by:

1. **Map the structure's beats to their approximate story positions** (using percentages or chapter estimates)
2. **Fill in the skeleton with the user's story details** from their premise
3. **Use placeholders** for beats the user hasn't developed yet
4. **Keep it skeletal** — resist the urge to elaborate beyond what the user has provided
5. **Mark unknowns** — note where the user has flexibility to decide later

Example skeleton format:

```markdown
# [Structure Name] Outline — [Working Title]

## Beat 1: [Name] (0-10%)
- What happens: [User's details or "TBD"]
- Character state: [Details]
- Notes: [Questions, possibilities]

## Beat 2: [Name] (10-25%)
- What happens: [Details]
...
```
