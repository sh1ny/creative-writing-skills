# Pitch Craft Reference

Deep guidance for the AI when developing pitch documents. These are craft notes — use them to generate better drafts and give more targeted feedback, not as checklists to read aloud.

---

## Logline

### The Job of a Logline

A logline must do four things in one sentence:
1. Establish who the story is about (specific protagonist, not a type)
2. Make the conflict visible (not implied)
3. Show what's at stake if they fail
4. Create irony, tension, or a compelling "what if"

### Core Formulas

**Conflict-first:**
> When [inciting incident], [specific protagonist] must [active external goal] or [concrete consequence] — but [antagonistic force/obstacle] stands in the way.

**Character-first:**
> [Specific protagonist with a defining quality] wants [goal], but [obstacle/opposition], and now [escalating stakes].

**Concept-first (high-concept):**
> In a world where [premise], [protagonist] must [goal] before [deadline/consequence].

### The Irony Test

The best loglines contain irony — the protagonist is uniquely wrong-footed for the situation they're in:
- A con artist must tell the truth to save his daughter
- A hitman falls in love with his next target's wife
- A grief counselor loses her will to live

Ask: is there a tension between WHO the protagonist is and WHAT they must do? If yes, the logline has irony. If no, push harder.

### Logline Diagnostics

| Symptom | Problem | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| "A young woman discovers her true destiny" | No specificity, no conflict | Name her, name the obstacle |
| "Must save the world" | Stakes too generic | What specifically is lost? Who is hurt? |
| "Learns the true meaning of family" | Internal journey only | What's the external conflict forcing this? |
| Two sentences | Too complex | One central conflict only |
| No protagonist | Concept-forward | Ground it in a specific person |
| Passive verb ("is forced to") | Weak agency | Give protagonist an active choice |

### Length

25–40 words is the target. Under 20 is usually too thin. Over 50 is a two-conflict problem.

## Elevator Pitch

### What It's For

The elevator pitch is a sales document. It answers: "Why should I read this?" It's not a summary — it's a seduction.

### Anatomy of a Strong Elevator Pitch

**Para 1 — The Hook + Setup**
- Open with a single punchy line (can be the logline, or a striking premise statement)
- Establish genre, tone, and world in 1-2 sentences
- Introduce the protagonist with specificity: not "a detective" but "a burned-out forensic accountant"

**Para 2 — The Conflict Engine**
- What does the protagonist want (external goal)?
- What do they need (internal, often contradicts the external)?
- What or who is opposing them, and why is that opposition compelling?
- What's the central dramatic question? (Will X achieve Y before Z?)

**Para 3 — Stakes + Hook**
- What happens if they fail? Make it specific and personal, not abstract
- Optional: comp titles ("for fans of X meets Y") — use sparingly, only if genuinely accurate
- Close with a line that creates urgency or intrigue — not a cliffhanger, but a pull

### Comp Titles

Use comps that:
- Were published in the last 5 years (for market relevance)
- Are in a similar genre and tone
- Give a sense of both style AND story (one comp for story, one for voice/tone)

Avoid: obscure titles, massive bestsellers ("It's like Harry Potter"), films instead of books unless tone-matching.

Format: "X meets Y" or "for readers of X and Y" or "in the vein of X but with Y"

### Tone Matching

The pitch should feel like the book. A dark thriller pitch should be taut. A cozy mystery should be warm. A literary novel should have sentence-level care. The voice of the pitch is a signal to the reader/editor about what they're getting.

## Story Premise Document

### What It's For

The premise document is internal scaffolding — for you, for collaborators, for an AI working on this project. It's longer and more reflective than a pitch.

### What to Include

**Premise** (2-3 paragraphs)
The full story concept: who, what, where, when, why it matters.

**Central Dramatic Question**
One sentence: "Will [protagonist] [achieve goal] despite [obstacle]?"

**Themes**
2-4 themes the story explores. Not morals or messages — questions. "What does loyalty cost?" not "Loyalty is important."

**Tone & Register**
How does this book feel? Spare and taut? Lush and immersive? Dark comedy? Lyrical grief? Give the agent/collaborator a feel for the reading experience.

**Target Audience**
Be specific: "Adult fantasy readers who enjoy found-family stories with morally complex politics" beats "fantasy fans."

**Comparable Titles**
2-3 books, with a brief note on why each is a comp.

## Synopsis

### Short Synopsis (1 page, ~500 words)

Tell the whole story, hit only the biggest beats.

**Required elements:**
- Who the protagonist is (one sentence)
- The inciting incident
- The central conflict established
- The midpoint shift (something changes the game)
- The darkest moment / all-is-lost
- The climax (what choice does the protagonist make?)
- The resolution (what's the new status quo?)

**Leave out:**
- Subplots (unless load-bearing)
- Secondary character arcs
- Scene-level detail
- Backstory

### Long Synopsis (5-10 pages, ~2500-5000 words)

Tell the whole story with enough detail that someone could understand every major decision.

**Required elements:**
- All of the above, plus:
- All significant subplots
- Character arc milestones (not just plot events)
- Key scenes described briefly (not summarized to abstraction)
- All major reveals and twists (synopses spoil everything)
- Every character who drives a plot thread

**Common failures:**
- Trailing off after the second act (writers get tired)
- Omitting the ending (always include it)
- Summarizing scenes instead of describing them ("they argue" vs "Marcus confronts Aria about the missing letters, and she lies to his face for the first time")
- Forgetting internal character change — plot events AND psychological shifts both need to be tracked

### Voice in Synopsis

Third person, present tense is standard. Stay consistent. Write in plain, clear prose — synopses aren't the place for stylistic flourishes.

One exception: if your novel's voice is very distinctive, a hint of it in the synopsis can work in your favor. Use judgment.

## When the User Has No Project Context Yet

If the user invokes `/pitch` without a `Meta/project.md` or any existing pitch files, they're entering the workflow here from scratch. In this case:

1. Ask for a premise in their own words — even rough, even half-formed. "Just tell me what the story is about."
2. Extract from their response: protagonist, central conflict, genre, tone signals
3. Generate a rough logline first — this forces clarity
4. Ask: "Does this capture it? What's wrong?" — iterate until they say yes
5. Then offer to expand into elevator pitch or premise doc

Don't demand structured input. Writers often know their story without knowing how to pitch it. Your job is to surface the pitch from what they already know.

## Internal vs External Goal (the Want/Need Split)

Almost every strong story has this structure:
- **External goal (want):** What the protagonist is consciously pursuing. Tangible, visible, achievable.
- **Internal goal (need):** What the protagonist actually needs to change/learn/accept. Often contradicts or complicates the want.

The pitch should reference both, even if briefly. The external goal drives plot. The internal need drives character arc.

Example:
- Want: recover the stolen artifact before the auction
- Need: learn to trust her team instead of doing everything alone

A pitch that only addresses the external goal feels hollow. A pitch that only addresses the internal journey feels plotless. Both together = a story.

## Questions to Ask When a Draft Isn't Working

If a draft logline or pitch feels flat, run through these:

1. **Is the protagonist specific?** Name + one defining quality minimum.
2. **Is there a visible antagonistic force?** Not just internal struggle — something that actively opposes the goal.
3. **Are the stakes personal?** "The world will end" is abstract. "She'll lose her daughter" is not.
4. **Is there irony or tension in the setup?** Who is this person, and why are they uniquely tested by this situation?
5. **Does the conflict create a question?** Can you phrase it as "Will X do Y before Z?" — if not, the conflict may be unclear.
6. **Is the genre legible?** A reader should be able to infer the genre from the pitch alone.
